Home watch vs. asking a neighbor: what actually gets missed.

The informal arrangement covers one kind of risk. Here is the category it does not touch — and why those are the expensive ones.

Almost everyone leaving for the winter has the same plan: a neighbor, a brother-in-law, or the guy across the street is going to keep an eye on the place. It is free, it is easy, and the person offering genuinely means it.

It also fails in a very specific and predictable way. Not because your neighbor is unreliable — because what you asked for and what you actually need are two different jobs.

What 'keeping an eye on it' usually means in practice

In our experience, the informal arrangement almost always converges on the same handful of behaviors: driving past a few times, grabbing mail and packages, and going inside if something looks obviously wrong from the street.

That covers exactly one category of risk — visible, exterior, obvious. Break-ins, storm damage to the front of the house, a garage door left open. Real risks, genuinely worth covering.

The problem is that the expensive failures in a Northeast Ohio winter are almost never visible from the street.

What gets missed

A slow leak. A supply line weeping behind a vanity or under a kitchen sink does not announce itself. It soaks the cabinet base, then the subfloor, then the joists. From outside, nothing. By the time it is visible, you are replacing flooring and cabinets and dealing with mold remediation.

Heat that is running but not heating. A furnace can run continuously and still fail to hold temperature — a failing igniter, a cracked heat exchanger locking out, a blower struggling. From the driveway the house looks completely normal. Inside, it is 42 degrees and the pipes in the exterior wall are counting down.

An ice dam building. Ice dams form over days and push meltwater back up under shingles and into the ceiling. Someone looking at the front of the house sees icicles, which read as normal winter, not as water entering the attic.

Pests moving in. Mice find an empty, heated house in November and treat it as a season-long residence. Nobody driving past sees droppings in the pantry.

The sump pump that stopped. A failed pump does nothing at all until the February thaw, and then it does everything at once, into a basement nobody has entered since Thanksgiving.

The other problem: you cannot really hold a favor accountable

This is the uncomfortable part, and it is worth naming plainly. When someone is doing you a favor, you cannot reasonably ask them to go every week on a schedule, walk a checklist, photograph the water heater, and text you a report.

So you do not ask. And they do their honest best, which is a drive-by when they think of it. Then in March there is a problem and everybody feels terrible — the neighbor because they feel responsible, you because you cannot be upset with someone who was doing you a kindness for free.

A paid service removes that entirely. It is a job with a scope, a schedule, and a deliverable.

What a professional home watch visit actually covers

  • Exterior walk-around — roofline, gutters, siding, windows, doors, storm and entry damage
  • Interior walk-through — every room, every level, checking for water, moisture, mold, and mildew
  • HVAC confirmed running and holding the set temperature
  • Water heater, visible plumbing, and electrical panel inspected
  • Fridge, freezer, and other running amenities confirmed working
  • Sump pump checked
  • Pest inspection
  • Mail, packages, and flyers collected so the home never looks empty
  • Photo report texted or emailed after every single visit
  • Same-day alert with an action plan if anything is wrong

The list is the point. It is the same every visit, which is what turns spot-checking into monitoring.

What it costs against what it prevents

Home watch plans are quoted by home size and visit frequency. Weekly is the standard through a Northeast Ohio winter.

Set that against the alternatives. A burst supply line discovered weeks later routinely runs into five figures once flooring, drywall, cabinetry, and remediation are counted. A failed sump in a finished basement is comparable. An ice dam that soaks a ceiling means drywall, insulation, paint, and possibly mold work.

Every one of those is a problem that a person standing in the house on the right Tuesday catches in the first week.

Where the neighbor still helps

Keep them. A neighbor who has your phone number, notices an unfamiliar vehicle, and can call you is genuinely valuable — that is a category of awareness no scheduled service replicates.

The right arrangement is both: a neighbor who watches the street, and a documented service that walks the inside. And if the same crew is already mowing your lawn in October and plowing your driveway in January, the property looks continuously lived-in from the road — which is its own form of security.

Common questions

Isn't asking a neighbor good enough?

For collecting mail and noticing something obviously wrong outside, it is genuinely useful. What it does not cover is the interior — slow leaks, a furnace running but not heating, a failed sump pump, pests. Those are the failures that produce large claims, and none of them are visible from the street.

How often should a home be checked in a Northeast Ohio winter?

Weekly is the standard and it is what we recommend. Frozen pipes, roof leaks, and heating failures cause exponentially more damage the longer they run unnoticed. The difference between a one-week and a one-month discovery is usually the difference between a repair and an insurance claim.

What do I get after each visit?

A photo report by text or email — what was checked, what was found, and anything needing attention. If something is wrong you hear about it the same day, with options for how to handle it, not a note waiting for you when you get home.

Can you handle the lawn and snow too?

That is the main advantage of using us. The same crew that checks the interior also mows in season, maintains the beds, and plows the driveway all winter. The property stays maintained and looks occupied from the street, which is one of the better burglary deterrents there is.
